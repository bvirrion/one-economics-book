\begin{solution}{exo:g12:exchange-rates-and-balance-of-payments:12}
\omterm{def:g12:exchange-rates-and-balance-of-payments:floating}{Floating exchange rates} vary far more than price levels from month to month, so real rates move with them; and deviations from parity take three to five years to halve. In
September 1992 sterling fell sharply in days, while British prices did not change.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:exchange-rates-and-balance-of-payments:13}
The euro member: a fixed rate and free movement of money, without its own interest rate. The United States: its own rate and free movement, with a floating rate. The third
country: a fixed rate and its own rate, by limiting the movement of money.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:g12:exchange-rates-and-balance-of-payments:14}
In the union the member's currency cannot fall and its central bank cannot cut the rate for it alone: output and employment fall, and stay low until its wages and prices fall
relative to its partners', workers move, or transfers arrive. With its own floating currency, the currency would depreciate, making its exports cheaper, and its central bank
could cut its rate.
\end{solution}

others, and can drain the reserves in days.
\end{solution}
